\section{Background}

\subsection{Proximal Policy Approximation}

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a first-order policy gradient method
that approximates the trust-region constraint of TRPO without requiring
second-order optimization. Instead of explicitly constraining the policy
update, PPO uses a clipped surrogate objective to keep the updated policy
close to one used to collect the rollouts
\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms}.

\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_t \Big[
\min\big(
r_t(\theta) A_t,\;
\mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) A_t
\big)
\Big]
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
r_t(\theta) =
\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_t}(a_t \mid s_t)},
\qquad
A_t = A^{\pi_{\theta_t}}(s_t,a_t).
\end{equation}

Due to its simplicity and strong performance in single-agent settings, PPO has
become a standard choice in many reinforcement learning tasks. 

\subsection{Indipendent PPO}

We extend this to \textbf{Independent Proximal Policy Optimization (IPPO)} by
instantiating a separate PPO learner for each agent. This allows agents to
optimize their own clipped objectives independently while treating other
agents as part of the environment, enabling PPO to handle cooperative
multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) tasks like Overcooked.

Under independent learning, a MARL problem is decomposed into $n$
interdependent learning problems, each solved independently by an agent that
learns its policy and value function based solely on local observations. While
this approach is simple and scalable, it violates the stationarity assumption
inherent to Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), since the environment dynamics
shift as other agents update their policies. Consequently, independent
learning can be affected by non-stationarity.

Centralized training methods attempt to address non-stationarity by using
global information during training, either through joint policy learning or
centralized value functions. However, recent empirical results show that IPPO
can match or even outperform CTDE methods, such as QMIX and MAPPO
\cite{dewitt2020independentlearningneedstarcraft,
yu2022surprisingeffectivenessppocooperative}.

The clipped surrogate objective of PPO effectively mitigates non-stationarity
issues, making IPPO a simple and effective baseline for cooperative MARL,
without requiring a centralized critic conditioned on the global state or on
the concatenation of agents observations, as in MAPPO.

\subsection{Reward Shaping}

The reward signal in Overcooked is extremely sparse: the environment assigns a
reward of 20 only when a soup is successfully delivered. However, a successful
delivery is a complex interaction requiring a long sequence of coordinated
actions. Specifically, an agent must collect three ingredients, move to the
pot, place the ingredients, start cooking, pick up a dish, return to the pot,
collect the soup, move to the delivery area, and finally deliver it. This must
be accomplished while avoiding invalid recipes, which result in zero reward.

Because this chain of operations is so long, reinforcement learning algorithms
struggle to figure out which intermediate steps actually contributed to the
final success. This significantly slows down learning.

By looking rendered frames and the environment code, we found that Overcooked
already implements a reward shaping mechanism, returned at each step through
the \textit{info} dictionary as a dense shaped reward
(\texttt{ep\_shaped\_r}). The environment assigns fixed positive values for
specific events such as ingredient placement in the pot ($3$), dish pickup
($3$), and soup pickup ($5$).

Although this reward-shaping scheme is relatively simple, it proved to be
highly effective. The use of a dense shaped reward enabled the learning
algorithm to quickly develop an understanding of how to prepare soups. We did
observe that this form of reward shaping can lead to \textit{reward hacking};
for example, early in training the agents repeatedly picked up and dropped
dishes to exploit the reward signal. However, these behaviors were transient.
As training progressed, the agents moved beyond such exploits and converged to
stable, effective policies that exhibited correct behavior.

Since these shaped rewards are generated per agent, we designed a Gymnasium
wrapper with two distinct modes to handle them:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shared Mode}: The dense rewards obtained by all agents are
        summed and distribuited equally. This means both agents get credit
        whenever a beneficial action happens, regardless of who did it.
    \item \textbf{Individual Mode}: The dense reward is assigned only to the
        agent that performed the action.
\end{itemize}

In our experiments, shared mode achieved faster convergence and higher final
total reward. Consequently, it can be interpreted as a team-based reward that
promotes coordinated learning.

\subsection{State Representation}

By default, the Overcooked environment returns a \textbf{featurized state}
observation, represented as a fixed-length vector of size 96 designed to
reduce state complexity. This hand-engineered encoding replaces the raw grid
with the agent's absolute position, orientation, and relative distances to key
entities such as the co-player, ingredients, and pots \cite{cai2021overcooked}.

In contrast, the \textbf{lossless state} encoding preserves the complete
spatial topology of the environment. It is structured as a tensor of shape
$W\times H\times26$, where $W$ and $H$ represent the layout dimensions. The 26
channels serve as semantic layers, with each channel acting as a binary mask
indicating the presence of specific objects at given coordinates. To
investigate the trade-off between engineered features and complete state
information, we modified the environment to selectively return either the
featurized state or the raw lossless tensor \cite{cai2021overcooked}.

\subsection{Architectures}

For featurized states, we employed a three-layer MLP with \texttt{tanh}
activations, consistent with the standard PPO2 implementation
\cite{huang2022ppo}.

For lossless states, we used a modified Nature-CNN
architecture~\cite{mnih2015humanlevel} restricted to kernel sizes of
$3\times3$ with a stride of $1$. Given the small spatial dimensions of the
layouts, larger kernels or strides would excessively downsample the feature
maps, collapsing the grid into a single cell and destroying local spatial
context.

All hidden layers and convolutions used \textit{orthogonal initialization}
(scale $\sqrt{2}$, bias $0$). The policy head weights were initialized with a
scale of 0.01 to ensure a uniform initial action distribution at start for
maximum exploration, while the value head used a scale of 1.0 to immediately
match the magnitude of expected returns \cite{huang2022ppo}.

We also explored sharing parts of the network between the policy and the value
function, either by sharing two MLP layers or by using a CNN backbone with
separate final policy and value heads. While this approach effectively
improves computational efficiency, since the shared layers are computed fewer
times,

In addition, we also explored the possibility of sharing some layers between
the policy and the value function. In particular, we experimented with sharing
either two MLP layers or a CNN backbone, followed by a single final layer
acting as a policy head and a value head. Although this approach effectively
improves computational efficiency—since the shared two or three layers are
computed only once, it negatively affects convergence and degrades the final
return achieved. This behavior is consistent with previous observations, as
the separate-networks architecture clearly outperforms the shared one in
simpler environments. The inferior performance of the shared-network
architecture is likely due to the competing objectives of the policy and value
functions, as discussed in \cite{huang2022ppo}.

\subsection{Experiments}

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/training_layouts}
    \caption{Training layouts: Cramped Room, Asymmetric Advantage,
    Coordination Ring, Counter Circuit, and Forced Coordination.}
    \label{fig:training_layouts}
\end{figure}

To understand the model capacity to generalize, we use a wrapper of the
Overcooked-AI environment that samples one layout from a list of layouts at
every reset. In this way, we hope the agent pair becomes invariant to the
layout and understands how to generalize.

We select 5 layouts for the \textbf{training set}: \textit{Cramped Room},
\textit{Asymmetric Advantage}, \textit{Coordination Ring}, \textit{Counter
Circuit}, and \textit{Forced Coordination} (Figure
\ref{fig:training_layouts}). We evaluate generalization on a separate
\textbf{test set}: \textit{Cramped Room Tomato} (introduces tomatoes) and a $5
\times 5$ (more open-space).

Since every layout has different dimensions in width and height, this presents
a problem for lossless encoding. Because the Nature-CNN inspired architecture
involves a flattening step after the convolutional layers, and the subsequent
fully connected layer requires fixed dimensions, it is necessary to pad each
layout to the maximum dimension among all layouts.

Every 50 batches during training, the agent is evaluated in \textbf{self-play}
on all 5 training layouts. Training runs for 10M environment steps resulting
in roughly 400 batches. Each layout is tested with 5 rollouts, and the total
reward across layouts is summed. The agent pair achieving the highest total
reward is saved. At the end of training, the best agent pair is reloaded and
evaluated again on all layouts, including the holdout set, using 10 rollouts
per layout.

In addition to the generalized agent pair, we also trained agent pairs on each
individual training layout. This let us see the maximum reward an agent can
achieve when it focuses solely on one layout, and gave us a baseline to
compare against the generalized pair. By looking at this comparison, we can
understand what the generalized agent learns across multiple layouts, and
whether trying to be versatile comes at the cost of performing optimally on
any single layout.

\subsubsection{Hardware \& Software} 

Experiments were conducted on a desktop machine equipped with a 16-core CPU,
32 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with 24GB of VRAM, used for
both rollout collection and training updates. We developed the implementation
using \textbf{Jax} and \textbf{Flax}, which helped significantly with
computation speed. It is important to note that a Jax implementation of the
environment, such as the one available in JaxMARL, would allow for much faster
rollout collection. This phase was the main bottleneck in our pipeline, as the
GPU often remained underutilized while waiting for the CPU to finish
collecting rollouts. Although we avoided switching to a Jax-native environment
to align with the specific requirements of the project, such a transition
would be incredibly beneficial to maximize computational throughput.

Furthermore, since the primary bottleneck is rollout collection, a
\textbf{vectorized environment} was utilized to run multiple environments in
parallel across separate processes using Gymnasium \texttt{AsyncVectorizeEnv}.
In this setting, the agent pair interacts simultaneously across $N$
environments, significantly increasing training throughput. The number of
environments was set to approximately the number of available CPU cores. It is
worth noting that a native Jax implementation would allow $N$ to be increased
by orders of magnitude.

By leveraging this setup, we achieved a training time of approximately 30
minutes per 10M steps. This allowed for extensive hyperparameter tuning with
Optuna within a highly reasonable timeframe.

\subsubsection{Hyperparameters Optimization}

We conducted hyperparameter optimization using \textbf{Optuna} over 50 trials,
with a total runtime of approximately 16 hours. The hyperparameter search used
the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) to guide sampling toward promising
regions of the search space by modeling the performance of good and bad
configurations, while Hyperband was employed to efficiently allocate resources
and prune underperforming trials through early stopping. Full details are
reported in Appendix \ref{appendix:hpo}.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:opt_history}, MLP models with featurized
environment encoding consistently outperformed CNNs with lossless environment
encoding, converging faster and achieving higher final returns.

The parallel coordinates plots (Figure~\ref{fig:parallel_coords}) show that
stable and high-performing configurations concentrate in a narrow region of
the hyperparameter space: an entropy coefficient of $0.01$–$0.05$, GAE
$\lambda$ between $0.9$ and $0.95$, 800-step rollout segments, and learning
rates of $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$. The MLP uses a 256-unit hidden layer. Sharing
parameters between agents consistently improved performance, whereas sharing a
backbone between the policy and value networks did not.

The results reported in Table~\ref{tab:agent_performance} demonstrate that
IPPO achieves robust performance. Notably, both the single-layout specialized
pairs and the multi-layout agents converge to high-reward policies on the
majority of training environments, successfully solving \textit{Cramped Room},
\textit{Asymmetric Advantage}, \textit{Coordination Ring}, and \textit{Forced
Coordination}.

However, \textit{Counter Circuit} remains unsolved, yielding zero reward for
all trained agent pairs. This failure appears to result from a specific
inability to adapt to ingredient changes; \textit{Counter Circuit} is the only
layout requiring the delivery of tomato-based dishes. The agents, however,
persist in behaviors related to onion-based recipes, failing to adjust to the
tomato availability.

Single-layout agent pairs show no form of generalization and fail completely
on new layouts. Even if the multi-layout agent pair has some form of
generalization and successfully solves the \textit{Cramped Tomato} layout, it
still persists with onion-based deliveries. Moreover, it struggles in more
complex environments with layouts never seen before.

\section{Discussion \& Conclusion}

Overall, the results indicate the emergence of non-trivial generalization
behavior. Training on a distribution of layouts enables agents to learn more
flexible policies that transfer to unseen environments, as evidenced by
performance on \textit{Cramped Tomato}. However, this generalization remains
limited and degrades rapidly when agents are evaluated on layouts slightly
more complex than those encountered during training.

The failure on \textit{Counter Circuit} highlights the influence of
layout-specific biases. This layout requires exclusively tomato-based
deliveries, whereas the learned policy remains biased toward onion-based
deliveries. We hypothesize that this stems from the fact that four of the five
training layouts involve onion-based deliveries (see Figure
\ref{fig:training_layouts}). Such over-representation likely biases the
policy, trapping agents in suboptimal behaviors, effectively ``overfitting''
to the training environment distribution. To achieve stronger generalization,
it appears crucial to extend training with randomized or procedurally
generated layouts to ensure a more uniform distribution of ingredients and map
structures. Since Overcooked-AI supports the integration of new layouts,
future work could explore whether increased diversity in training environments
improves transfer performance.

In conclusion, these results show that multi-layout training can improve
policy generalization, and IPPO proves highly effective even in multi-agent
settings. Nonetheless, generalization to unseen scenarios remains challenging,
motivating future work on more diverse training environments, refined reward
shaping, and other strategies that improve adaptability to novel layouts.
